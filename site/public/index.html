One codebase, explained three times

The same system explained to the three people who need it: the engineer who inherits the code, the person who uses the product to get a job done, and the executive deciding whether to back it. Not one document with three audiences bolted on, but three packs that differ in what they say, how deep they go, and what they show.

And the documentation the codebase already had

The packs are written for people. This is different: it is the working documentation the repository carries in its own tree, rendered so it can be read in a browser. Two hundred and eighteen pages, and four hundred and fifty-six diagrams drawn from mermaid sources held beside the code.

It is here because a pack is a route through a system and this is the map underneath it. An engineer who wants the part a chapter summarised in a paragraph will find it written out at length, with the files it was checked against and the commit at which that was true. Each diagram shows the drawing and keeps its source one click below.

Made here, on our own hardware

No part of this content was written by a hosted model. The chapters, the diagrams, the narration and the clips were produced by a large language model running on a GPU in our own rack, reading a private repository that never left it.

WhatHow it was made
ChaptersA 27-billion-parameter model on a local GPU, reading the source and citing the lines it read
DiagramsDrawn as code by the same model, rendered locally, then measured for legibility at the size they are seen
ScreenshotsCaptured from the running product by a browser on the same network, never a mock-up
NarrationSynthesised by a local speech model; every scene timed to the audio rather than estimated
IllustrationAn image-to-video model on a local GPU, labelled as illustration wherever it appears
AssemblyA deterministic compositor and FFmpeg, on the same machine

The reason is not cost. A codebase under a confidentiality agreement cannot be posted to someone else's inference endpoint, and an explainer worth having must read the whole thing. Keeping the model on the same network as the code is what makes the work possible at all.

How a pack is built

Each pack is produced by a sequence of bounded steps rather than one long conversation. A step writes one chapter, or draws one diagram, or takes the screenshots, and then stops; the next step reads what it left on disk. Every claim about behaviour cites the file and lines it came from, and an independent step opens each citation and judges whether the code actually says what the sentence claims.

Media is made, then looked at, then revised. The looking is done by a separate step that is handed the frames, because a model cannot see a picture it has only written to disk. That step has rejected screenshots showing a product mid-failure, diagrams whose labels were six pixels tall, and a title band colliding with a heading underneath it.

What the packs do not claim. Each one ends with what its gates actually showed, including what is still red. A pack that passed every check says so; a pack with an unverified claim says that instead.

Access

The passphrase is shared separately and is not recorded here. Content is sealed with AES-256-GCM under a key derived from that passphrase, and the key derivation is deliberately slow. Nothing on this host is readable without it, including the clips, the captions and the list of what each pack contains.
